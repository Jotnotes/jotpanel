# Being a reseller

You have been given an account on somebody else's machine, and that account can take on customers of
its own. This is what you can do, what you cannot, and why.

---

## What you hold

Your provider gave you a package, which is a set of ceilings: so many websites, so many mailboxes, so
many databases, so much disk. Everything you do sits inside those numbers.

You cannot raise them yourself. Nobody can raise their own limits in this panel, including the person
who owns the machine. If you need more, you ask your provider, and that is a conversation rather than
a setting.

## What you can do

**Define packages of your own to sell**, out of what you hold. You can slice your ten websites into
five customers with two each, or one customer with ten. What you cannot do is sell eleven, and the
refusal says so with both numbers in it.

**Take on customers.** Each gets an account and an organization of its own, under you and only you.

**Act on your customers' resources.** Their websites, their mail, their databases, their files. This
is not a read-only view: you can add an alias to a customer's site and the web server on the machine
changes.

**Suspend and restore a customer.** A suspended customer's domains close and their sign-in is
refused, and putting them back reopens both. Nothing is deleted by a suspension.

## What you cannot do

**See or touch anybody else's customers.** Not the other resellers' and not your provider's. This is
enforced on ownership records rather than by hiding things on a screen, so there is no address you
can type to get around it. Twenty-three forbidden paths were tried on purpose against a live machine
and all twenty-three were refused.

**Reach the machine itself.** The firewall, the services, the operating system's updates and the
machine's own certificates belong to whoever owns the box.

**See your provider's costs**, their other customers, or anything about their business.

## The approval loop applies to you too

Everything you do is proposed, approved and recorded, with your name on it. Your provider can see
that record, because it is their machine. Your customers see their own.

That cuts both ways and mostly in your favour. When a customer says a mailbox was deleted, the record
says whether it was, who did it and when.

## Getting your customers out

A whole account leaves as one ZIP, with the credentials inside it encrypted under a passphrase you
choose. You do not need anybody's permission to do it and you do not have to ask.
